How to read your dog’s body language: 12 signals to notice

Dogs communicate with their posture, face, movement and distance—not just with barking or growling. Learning to notice small changes can help you make walks, greetings, grooming and family life safer and more comfortable.

Published November 22, 2026 · General educational information

A wagging tail is often treated as a universal sign of happiness, but dog communication is more complicated. A dog can wag while excited, uncertain or uncomfortable. A yawn may mean tiredness—or it may appear during a stressful interaction. The meaning depends on the dog, the situation and the rest of the body.

Use this guide as a way to observe, not as a test that can diagnose exactly what a dog feels. Look for several signals together, compare them with your dog’s usual behaviour, and give the dog room to move away whenever possible.

A quick whole-body scan
  • Is the body loose and moving naturally, or stiff and held still?
  • Is the dog’s weight balanced, leaning forward, or shifted backwards?
  • Are the eyes and mouth soft, or tight and fixed?
  • Are the ears and tail in a natural position for this individual dog?
  • Can the dog choose to leave, or is someone blocking the way?

One sign alone rarely tells the whole story. Context and combinations matter.

1. Twelve body-language signals to notice

1. A loose, relaxed body

A comfortable dog often moves freely, with muscles that look soft rather than rigid. The mouth may rest slightly open, the eyes look soft, and the ears sit in a natural position. The dog can shift position, sniff or settle without looking frozen in place. Compare this with your own dog’s normal posture; some breeds naturally carry their tails or ears differently.

2. A play bow

A dog that lowers the front half of the body while keeping the rear raised may be inviting play, especially when the movement is loose and bouncy. Look for a relaxed face and repeated pauses. Healthy play usually includes give-and-take: dogs may take turns chasing or briefly stop and re-engage. If one dog repeatedly tries to escape or hide, end the interaction calmly.

3. A wagging tail

A wag tells you that the dog is emotionally activated; it does not, by itself, prove friendliness. Notice the tail’s height, speed, stiffness and movement along with the dog’s posture, face and approach. A loose, whole-body wiggle can look very different from a high, rigid tail with a tense body. Do not approach an unfamiliar dog just because its tail is moving.

4. Turning the head or body away

A dog may turn away, angle the body sideways or avoid looking directly at a person or another dog when the interaction feels uncomfortable. This can be a request for more space. Do not follow the dog’s face with your hand or keep stepping closer. Pause and allow the dog to choose whether to return.

5. Lip-licking when no food is present

A quick tongue flick or repeated lip-licking can occur when a dog is unsure. Check what happened immediately beforehand: perhaps someone leaned over the dog, reached toward its head or moved close to a valued item. Lip-licking can also have ordinary causes, so look for other changes rather than interpreting it in isolation.

6. Yawning outside a sleepy moment

Yawning after waking or before a nap is normal. A yawn during handling, a crowded greeting or a tense encounter may be another sign that the dog is uncomfortable. If it appears with turning away, a tight mouth or a lowered posture, stop the activity and give the dog a break.

7. Lifting one front paw

A brief paw lift can be a sign of uncertainty, particularly when the dog is also still, looking away or holding its body low. It is not a reliable emotion label on its own: dogs may lift a paw for other reasons, including pain or a learned behaviour. If the paw lift is new, persistent or accompanied by limping, contact a veterinarian.

8. Crouching or tucking the tail

A lowered body, head held down, ears drawn back and tail tucked close to the belly can indicate fear or discomfort. Some dogs become very still; others try to retreat. Do not corner, grab or force a frightened dog to greet someone. Increase distance and make sure there is a clear escape route.

9. Showing the whites of the eyes

Sometimes called “whale eye,” this can happen when a dog turns its head away but keeps its eyes fixed on something, making more of the white visible. It can be a warning that the dog is uneasy, especially when paired with a tense face or still body. Avoid reaching toward the dog or taking away its space.

10. Freezing or becoming suddenly stiff

A sudden pause, closed mouth, rigid body or fixed stare deserves attention. Freezing can happen before a dog moves away, growls or snaps. Do not test the dog by moving closer. Stop what you are doing, turn slightly sideways and calmly create more space.

11. Raised hair along the back

Raised hackles are an automatic body response that can occur with excitement, fear, surprise or conflict. They do not prove that a dog is aggressive. Treat them as a reason to look at the full picture: is the dog loose and playful, or stiff, staring and trying to create distance?

12. Growling, snarling or showing teeth

These are clear signals to stop and give the dog space. A growl is useful information that the dog is uncomfortable; it is not a moment to scold or challenge the animal. Calmly end the interaction, prevent people from moving closer, and work out what made the dog feel it needed to warn you.

Important: These signals are clues, not a fixed dictionary. A dog’s age, health, past experiences, breed-related body shape and the immediate environment can all affect what you see.

2. How to respond when a dog seems uncomfortable

  1. Pause the interaction. Stop petting, grooming, hugging or asking the dog to approach. Avoid adding more pressure.
  2. Create space. Turn your body slightly sideways, keep your movements calm and give the dog a clear path to retreat. Do not corner it or block a doorway.
  3. Keep your hands to yourself. Do not reach over the dog’s head, grab its collar or try to hold it still to “show there is nothing to fear.”
  4. Let the dog choose. If the dog moves away, allow that choice. Do not follow, lure it back into the same uncomfortable situation or insist on a greeting.
  5. Notice the trigger. Make a simple note of what happened just before the signal: a stranger approaching, another dog, a loud sound, handling a sore area or a valued toy being moved.

For an unfamiliar dog, ask the owner before approaching. If the dog does not move toward you with a loose, comfortable body, admire it from a distance. Never assume that a dog wants to be touched because it is sitting quietly or wagging its tail.

If a dog growls or snaps: Stop the interaction and calmly move people away. Do not shout, punish, hit or force contact. Keep children and other pets separated while you make a safe plan. If there is an immediate risk of injury, prioritise distance and contact local animal-control or emergency services as appropriate.

3. Safer greetings around children and visitors

Children and adults should be taught to leave dogs alone when they are eating, sleeping, caring for puppies, hiding or resting in their own safe space. Do not let anyone hug, climb on, chase or disturb a dog to get a reaction. Even a familiar family dog needs supervision around children; never leave a young child alone with a dog.

Before a visitor arrives, give the dog a quiet room, barrier or comfortable retreat where nobody will follow it. Ask visitors to ignore the dog at first. If the dog chooses to approach, they can remain calm and let the dog sniff without reaching over it. The dog should always be free to leave.

4. When to seek professional help

Speak with a veterinarian if your dog’s behaviour changes suddenly, if handling has become difficult, or if you notice signs that could also indicate pain or illness. A vet can check for medical causes before you assume the behaviour is purely training-related.

If fear, growling, snapping or repeated distress continues, ask your veterinarian about referral to a qualified, reward-based behaviour professional or veterinary behaviourist. Avoid anyone who promises a guaranteed fix or recommends frightening, painful or confrontational methods.

5. Frequently asked questions

Does a wagging tail mean a dog is friendly?

No. A wagging tail shows movement and arousal, not a guaranteed invitation to approach. Read the tail together with posture, face, movement and context.

Should I punish my dog for growling?

No. Stop the interaction and create distance. Punishing a growl can suppress a warning without resolving the fear or discomfort behind it. Ask a veterinarian or qualified behaviour professional for help if it happens repeatedly.

Can a dog look relaxed but still be uncomfortable?

Yes. Signals can be subtle and brief, and some dogs become very still rather than visibly struggling. Watch for changes from that individual dog’s normal behaviour and give them the option to move away.

What if my dog suddenly avoids being touched?

Arrange a veterinary check, especially if the change is new or focused on one area of the body. Pain, skin or ear problems and other medical issues can change how a dog responds to handling.
