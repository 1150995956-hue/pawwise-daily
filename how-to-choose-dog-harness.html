How to Choose a Dog Harness

A comfortable harness should fit your dog’s body, allow natural movement and stay secure on walks. Use this guide to compare styles, measure carefully and check the fit before every outing.

A well-fitted harness should stay secure without rubbing or limiting your dog’s movement. Photo: Jonathan Cooper / Pexels.

Quick answer: Measure your dog rather than guessing by breed or weight. Choose a harness that sits securely around the chest, does not press into the throat or armpits, and lets your dog walk naturally. Always check the manufacturer’s size chart.

1. Compare common dog harness styles

Y-front or chest-shaped harnesses

These are designed with straps that form a shape around the front of the chest. Look for a design that leaves the shoulder area free to move and does not press across the throat. The label alone does not guarantee a good fit: check how the specific harness sits on your dog.

Step-in harnesses

Your dog places its front paws through openings before the harness is fastened around the back. This can be convenient for dogs that dislike having equipment slipped over their head. Check that the straps stay in position and that your dog cannot back out of it.

Vest or padded harnesses

These spread contact across a broader area and may suit some dogs, but thick padding can become warm or rub if the shape is wrong. Check the fit around the chest and behind the front legs, especially in warm weather.

Escape-resistant designs

Some harnesses include an additional strap around the waist or extra adjustment points. They may be worth considering for dogs that can reverse out of ordinary harnesses. No design should be treated as escape-proof; check every buckle and fit before walking.

2. Measure your dog before choosing a size

  1. Use a flexible measuring tape and keep your dog standing naturally.
  2. Measure the chest girth around the widest part of the rib cage, usually just behind the front legs.
  3. Measure the neck only if the manufacturer's chart requests it, and follow its exact measuring position.
  4. Compare the measurements with that brand's size chart. Sizes are not consistent between manufacturers.
  5. If your dog falls between sizes, follow the brand's fitting guidance or contact the maker before ordering.

Weight and breed can be useful background information, but they cannot replace measurements. Two dogs of the same breed can have very different chest shapes, coat thickness and proportions.

3. Check comfort and fit after putting it on

  • Secure, not restrictive: The harness should not squeeze the rib cage or make breathing uncomfortable.
  • Keep the throat clear: Straps should not ride up and press against the neck when the leash becomes taut.
  • Watch the armpits: Straps that sit too close to the front legs can rub during walking.
  • Allow natural movement: Watch your dog walk, turn and sit. Look for shortened steps, awkward shoulder movement or reluctance.
  • Check for rubbing: After a short walk, inspect the skin and coat under the straps for redness, hair loss or damp, irritated areas.
  • Check security: The harness should not twist, slide over the head or allow your dog to back out.
Important: Do not rely on a universal “two-finger rule” as the only fit test. Harness construction differs. Follow the manufacturer's fitting instructions and make sure the harness is secure without restricting breathing or movement.

4. Understand front and back leash attachments

A back attachment is commonly used for everyday walking when a dog is comfortable on leash. A front attachment may help some handlers guide a dog that pulls by changing the direction of the leash, but it is not a substitute for training and can affect movement if used incorrectly.

Some harnesses offer both attachment points. Choose based on your dog's behaviour, body shape and your ability to handle the leash safely. If your dog lunges, is very strong, or has neck, back or mobility problems, ask a veterinarian or qualified force-free trainer for individual advice.

5. Safety and care checks

  • Inspect stitching, buckles, adjustment sliders and leash rings regularly.
  • Replace a harness with cracked hardware, frayed straps or damaged fasteners.
  • Choose reflective details or add visibility accessories for walks in low light.
  • Keep identification details current; a harness is not a substitute for a collar tag or microchip where appropriate.
  • Remove the harness when it is not needed unless its maker or your veterinarian advises otherwise. Check for trapped moisture and rubbing.
  • Introduce a new harness gradually with treats and calm praise. Never force a frightened dog into it.

6. Buying mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing by breed name or weight without measuring chest girth.
  • Assuming a padded or expensive harness must be more comfortable.
  • Ignoring the space behind the front legs, where rubbing can occur.
  • Buying a harness that is easy to put on but easy for the dog to escape from.
  • Using a front-clip attachment as the only solution to pulling.
  • Leaving a wet or dirty harness on without checking the skin underneath.

Frequently asked questions

How do I measure my dog for a harness?

Use a soft measuring tape and follow the brand's chart. Most harnesses use chest girth around the widest part of the rib cage, just behind the front legs. Some also require a neck measurement.

Should a dog harness be tight or loose?

It should be secure without squeezing, rubbing or restricting movement. Follow the maker's instructions and check your dog's skin and coat after use.

Is a front-clip harness better for pulling?

A front attachment may help some handlers redirect a dog, but it does not teach loose-leash walking on its own. Reward-based training and safe handling are still important.

Can a dog wear a harness all day?

Many harnesses are intended for walks rather than continuous wear. Remove it when it is not needed unless the maker or your veterinarian advises otherwise, and check for rubbing or trapped moisture.

Final checklist before you buy

Measure your dog, compare the maker's chart, inspect the harness shape around the chest and shoulders, check the adjustment range and hardware, and test the fit with a short supervised walk. If your dog moves awkwardly, rubs, freezes or tries to escape, stop and reassess the design or fit.

This guide is general educational information, not veterinary or training advice. Ask a veterinarian or qualified humane trainer about your dog's individual needs.
