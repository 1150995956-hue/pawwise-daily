How to introduce a new cat to your resident cat

Bringing another cat home can be exciting, but an immediate face-to-face meeting may be frightening for both animals. A slower introduction lets them become familiar with each other’s scent and presence while keeping daily life predictable.

Published November 21, 2026 · Pawwise Daily Editorial Team. This educational guide is based on published feline-behaviour guidance and is not a substitute for advice from your veterinarian.

Before the new cat arrives

Prepare a quiet transition room with a secure door. Set it up with the new cat’s own food and water bowls, litter tray, bedding, scratching surface, toys and hiding places. Include a comfortable perch if possible. Keep the resident cat’s familiar food, resting places and routine available elsewhere in the home.

Arrange a veterinary health check and ask whether either cat needs testing, parasite treatment or a period of separation before contact. If either cat is unwell, get veterinary advice before beginning introductions.

Step 1: Let the newcomer settle in

Bring the new cat directly to the prepared room and close the door before opening the carrier. Let the cat decide when to come out. Avoid pulling the cat from a hiding place or carrying the newcomer around the home. Visit calmly and allow the cat to approach at its own pace.

Step 2: Exchange scents

When both cats are settled, swap a small blanket or bedding item between their spaces. Let each cat investigate voluntarily. You can also use a soft cloth gently rubbed around one cat’s cheek area and place it in the other cat’s room. Pair the scent with something pleasant, such as a meal or a favourite treat, if both cats are comfortable.

If either cat hisses, avoids the item, stops eating or seems persistently unsettled, increase distance and give them more time. Do not force them to sniff each other.

Step 3: Build positive experiences near the closed door

Offer meals, treats or calm play on each side of the closed door, starting far enough away that both cats can relax. Over time, and only while both remain comfortable, you can move the activities a little closer. The aim is to help each cat associate the other’s sounds and scent with ordinary, positive moments.

Step 4: Try brief visual contact through a secure barrier

When the cats are relaxed with scent and sounds, allow them to see one another through a secure screen, gate or other barrier that neither cat can climb over or push through. Keep the first sessions short. Give both cats room to move away and reward calm behaviour. Never trap one cat in a carrier for the other to approach.

Step 5: Arrange short, supervised meetings

Only when barrier sessions are calm should you try a brief meeting in a space with escape routes and separate resting places. Keep sessions positive and end them before either cat becomes overwhelmed. Gradually increase the time together as repeated meetings remain relaxed. Do not leave the cats together unsupervised until they have shown consistent, peaceful interactions.

Make enough resources available

Provide multiple, separated places to eat, drink, rest, hide, scratch and use the litter tray. A common starting point for litter trays is one per cat plus one extra, placed in different accessible locations. Avoid forcing the cats to share a narrow passage or compete for a single important resource.

Signs you should slow down

  • Persistent hissing, growling, swatting or chasing.
  • One cat blocking doorways, food, water or litter access.
  • Repeated hiding, reduced appetite or unusual toileting.
  • Staring, crouching or an inability to settle after a session.

If tension appears, calmly separate the cats without putting your hands between them, then return to an earlier step. Do not punish either cat. If there are injuries, ongoing conflict, changes in eating or toileting, or either cat seems distressed, contact your veterinarian or a qualified feline behaviour professional.

Remember: There is no fixed introduction timetable. Some cats adjust quickly; others need weeks or longer. Progress should be based on each cat’s behaviour, not a deadline. The goal is safe, comfortable coexistence—not forcing the cats to become friends.
