Dog separation anxiety: signs and gentle ways to help

If your dog barks, paces, chews or seems distressed when you leave, they may be struggling with being alone. The first step is to understand what is happening—not to punish the behaviour.

Published November 19, 2026 · Pawwise Daily Editorial Team

Some dogs settle comfortably when left at home. Others find separation difficult and may show signs of distress soon after a person leaves. Separation-related behaviour is not spite or a dog trying to get revenge. It is a sign that the dog may be struggling to cope.

Not every bark, accident or chewed object means separation anxiety. Noise outside, incomplete toilet training, boredom, pain and other health or behaviour concerns can look similar. Look for a pattern and, when in doubt, ask a veterinarian to help identify the cause.

1. Signs your dog may be distressed when alone

Possible signs include persistent barking, whining or howling; pacing or being unable to settle; panting, trembling or drooling; scratching or chewing near doors and windows; attempts to escape; toileting indoors despite being house-trained; or refusing food that they normally enjoy. Some dogs show distress before the person has even left, when they notice keys, shoes or other departure cues.

These signs matter most when they reliably happen during separation and are not explained by another trigger. A dog that destroys one cushion while people are home may have a different problem from a dog that repeatedly scratches the exit door only after everyone leaves.

2. Find out what happens after you leave

If it is safe and practical, use a camera or an old phone placed securely to record a short period after departure. Watch for when signs begin, what seems to trigger them, whether your dog can eat or rest, and whether they recover. Do not leave a dog in a situation that is already causing severe distress just to collect a recording.

Keep a simple note of the time you leave, how long your dog remains settled, the first sign of stress and any outside events such as delivery noise. This information can help your veterinarian or behaviour professional.

3. Start with very small, comfortable steps

The aim is not to make your dog endure a long absence. It is to practise separations so brief that your dog can remain relaxed, then build gradually at a pace they can handle.

  1. Choose a comfortable resting area. Offer water, familiar bedding and safe activities. A crate is not automatically the answer; if confinement causes panting, frantic escape attempts or more distress, do not force it.
  2. Notice your dog’s calm baseline. Practise short periods of relaxing on a bed while you are still at home. Reward calm behaviour and let your dog choose to rest.
  3. Make departure signals less worrying. If keys or shoes trigger anxiety, sometimes pick them up without leaving. Keep these practice moments easy and stop if your dog becomes worried.
  4. Begin with a tiny separation. Step behind an interior door or briefly out of sight, then return while your dog is still calm. For some dogs this may be only a second or two.
  5. Increase only when your dog stays relaxed. Repeat easy steps and change the duration gradually. If you see stress, make the next practice shorter or easier. There is no universal timetable.
  6. Use food toys only if your dog is comfortable eating. A safe food puzzle can make a mild separation more pleasant for some dogs, but it is not a cure and may not help a dog who is too anxious to eat.
Remember: Progress is measured by relaxed behaviour, not by how many minutes you can stay away. A few calm seconds are a better starting point than a long, frightening absence.

4. Avoid responses that can make things worse

  • Do not punish barking, chewing or toileting after you return. Your dog may become more frightened without understanding what you are upset about.
  • Do not use a “cry it out” approach or deliberately leave your dog to panic.
  • Do not suddenly increase alone time because one practice session went well.
  • Do not assume extra exercise alone will resolve separation anxiety. Exercise and enrichment support wellbeing, but they do not replace an appropriate behaviour plan.
  • Do not give human medication, sedatives or supplements unless your veterinarian has advised it.

5. When to contact a veterinarian

Arrange a veterinary appointment if the behaviour is new, persistent, getting worse or accompanied by changes in appetite, toileting, sleep, movement or general health. Seek help promptly if your dog injures themselves trying to escape, breaks teeth or nails, becomes extremely distressed, or cannot settle even during very short separations.

A veterinarian can check for medical contributors and, when needed, refer you to a qualified veterinary behaviourist or appropriately credentialed behaviour professional. Moderate or severe cases often need an individual plan; medication may sometimes be considered by a veterinarian as part of treatment.

Urgent safety: If your dog has injured themselves, is struggling to breathe, collapses or may have swallowed something toxic, seek urgent veterinary care.

6. Frequently asked questions

Will my dog grow out of separation anxiety?

Do not rely on the problem disappearing by itself. Early support can prevent repeated distress and help identify the right approach.

Should I get another dog to keep mine company?

Not as a first-line fix. Separation distress is often linked to being apart from a particular person, so another pet may not solve it. Consider another animal only if you independently want and can care for one.

Is crate training always helpful?

No. Some dogs enjoy a crate when they have learned to feel safe there; others become more distressed when confined. Watch your dog’s response and discuss alternatives with a professional.

How long does improvement take?

There is no reliable one-size-fits-all timeline. It depends on the dog, the severity of distress, the environment and how consistently the plan can keep practice below the dog’s stress threshold.

This article is general educational information, not a diagnosis or treatment plan. A veterinarian who knows your dog can provide individual guidance.
