How to protect your dog’s paws in winter: snow, ice and road salt

Winter walks can be wonderful, but snow, sharp ice, wet fur and sidewalk de-icers can irritate or injure a dog’s feet. A simple before-and-after routine—and the right protection for your dog—can make cold-weather outings safer and more comfortable.

Published November 22, 2026 · Pawwise Daily Editorial Team

Quick answer: Choose a route with less visible salt and ice, use well-fitting dog boots when conditions call for them, and check every paw after each winter walk. Wipe or rinse away salt and de-icer with lukewarm water, dry between the toes, and remove snow gently. If your dog is limping, repeatedly lifting a paw, shivering or reluctant to walk, head indoors and reassess the conditions.

Why winter can be hard on dog paws

A dog’s paw pads are tough, but they are not invulnerable. Winter walks can expose feet to several hazards at once:

  • Road salt and de-icers: Ice-melting products can irritate paw pads and the skin between the toes. A dog may also lick residue from its feet after a walk. Depending on the ingredient and amount swallowed, ingestion can cause stomach upset or more serious problems.
  • Sharp or uneven ice: Frozen, jagged surfaces can cut pads or catch on nails. Icy paths can also make dogs slip.
  • Snow packed between toes: Wet snow can form hard clumps in the fur and between the pads. These clumps can be painful and may tempt a dog to chew at its feet.
  • Cold and dampness: Prolonged exposure can contribute to dry, cracked pads and, in severe conditions, frostbite or hypothermia.

Cold tolerance is individual. Size, age, health, body condition, coat, acclimation and the weather all matter. Puppies, senior dogs, small dogs, thin-coated dogs and pets with medical conditions may need shorter outings or extra protection. There is no single temperature that is safe for every dog.

Before you leave home

A quick check can prevent many problems:

  1. Look at the route. Choose cleared paths that are not visibly covered in salt or chemical melt when possible. Avoid slushy roadside puddles and piles of treated snow.
  2. Check each foot. Look for existing cracks, cuts, redness, swelling or debris between the toes. If a paw is already sore, ask your veterinarian before taking a long walk.
  3. Check the fur around the pads. Longer hair between the toes can collect snow. If it repeatedly forms ice balls, ask a groomer or veterinary professional to trim the excess hair carefully and evenly. Do not cut close to the skin or trim the pads.
  4. Prepare a towel. Keep a clean towel and a shallow bowl of lukewarm water near the door so you can clean and dry paws as soon as you return.
  5. Shorten the outing if needed. Watch your dog rather than relying only on the thermometer. If your dog seems uncomfortable, turn back.

Do dogs need winter boots?

Not every dog needs boots for every winter walk. However, a well-fitting pair can create a physical barrier between the paws and sharp ice, snow, wet ground and treated sidewalks. Boots may be especially useful for dogs who walk on salted city streets, spend longer periods outdoors, or develop repeated irritation in winter.

Look for boots that are:

  • Available in a size that matches your dog’s paw measurements
  • Snug enough to stay on but not tight around the leg or toes
  • Flexible, durable and suitable for the surface and weather
  • Easy to put on, remove and clean
  • Designed with a sole that offers useful grip without making the dog unstable

Introduce boots indoors before using them outside. Let your dog sniff them, reward calm interest, then try one boot for a few seconds. Gradually build up to all four boots and a short indoor walk. Use treats and praise; do not force a frightened dog to keep them on.

After the first few outings, check for rubbing, dampness, redness or pressure marks. Remove the boots when you get indoors, and clean and dry them according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A boot that twists, slips or causes discomfort is not protecting the paw properly.

When paw balm may help

A dog-specific paw balm may help moisturize dry pads and provide a temporary barrier against dampness and some surface irritants. Apply only as directed on the product label. If your dog has allergies, open sores, a known skin condition or a habit of licking products, ask your veterinarian which ingredients are appropriate.

Balm is not a substitute for cleaning paws after a walk, and it cannot prevent cuts from sharp ice. It is also not a reason to let a dog walk through visibly contaminated slush. If you use balm, still wipe or rinse the paws when you return home.

A safe after-walk paw routine

Make this a habit after walks on snow, ice or treated sidewalks:

  1. Check all four paws. Look at the pads, between each toe, around the nails and up the lower legs. Watch for ice, salt crystals, grit, cuts or redness.
  2. Wipe or rinse away residue. Use a damp, soft cloth or lukewarm water. Gently separate the toes so you can clean between the pads. Avoid harsh cleaners, alcohol, household disinfectants and strongly scented products.
  3. Remove snow gently. Let small clumps soften with a lukewarm, damp cloth. Do not pull, pick or cut hard ice balls out of the fur; pulling can hurt the skin.
  4. Dry thoroughly. Pat the paws dry, including the spaces between the toes. Damp fur can stay uncomfortable and encourage irritation.
  5. Look again. If your dog keeps licking one paw, limps, or reacts when you touch a foot, do not assume it is just salt. Check for an injury and contact your veterinarian if the problem persists or looks painful.

How to remove snow and ice between toes

Snowballs often form when wet snow sticks to longer hair around the feet. Bring your dog inside and use a lukewarm, damp cloth to soften the clumps. Gently massage the fur until the snow loosens, then dry the paw. Never use scissors close to the skin to cut out a frozen clump. If the fur is tightly matted or the paw is painful, let a groomer or veterinary professional help.

For dogs with longer furnishings around the feet—including some Westies—regular, careful maintenance of the hair between the pads can reduce snow buildup. Keep the natural coat intact for warmth and ask a groomer familiar with the breed to show you a safe trim. See our Westie care guide for more breed-specific grooming basics.

Signs your dog needs help

Stop the walk and move your dog to a warm, dry place if you notice paw lifting, reluctance to walk, shivering, whining, a tucked tail or sudden anxious behavior. These can be signs that your dog is not tolerating the cold.

Contact a veterinarian promptly for persistent limping, bleeding, a deep cut, swelling, cracked or raw pads, marked redness, discharge, or repeated licking and chewing. If you suspect frostbite or hypothermia, seek veterinary care immediately. Frostbite can be difficult to assess at first, and the full extent of injury may not be obvious right away.

Possible de-icer ingestion: If your dog licks or eats an ice-melting product, do not wait for symptoms and do not try to make your dog vomit unless a veterinarian tells you to. Contact your veterinarian or an animal poison-control service immediately, and keep the product packaging or ingredient list available. In the United States, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center can be reached at (888) 426-4435; consultation fees may apply.

Frequently asked questions

Should dogs wear boots in snow?

Some dogs do well without them on short, clean walks, while others benefit from boots on salted sidewalks, sharp ice or longer outings. Fit and comfort matter more than simply putting boots on every dog. Introduce them gradually and check the skin after use.

Is “pet-safe” ice melt completely safe for dogs?

No ice melt should be treated as food or allowed to remain on a dog’s paws. Products marketed as pet-friendly may use ingredients considered less hazardous in some circumstances, but they can still cause irritation or stomach upset if swallowed. Follow the label, store the product securely and clean your dog’s feet after walks.

Can I use paw balm instead of dog boots?

For a short walk in mild conditions, a suitable balm may help protect dry pads. It does not provide the same physical barrier as a boot and cannot protect against sharp ice. Choose protection based on the route, weather and your dog’s needs.

How often should I wash my dog’s paws in winter?

After every walk where your dog may have stepped in road salt, de-icer, slush or dirty snow, wipe or rinse the paws and dry them well. On a clean, dry route, a visual check may be enough, but inspect between the toes regularly.

How cold is too cold for a dog’s paws?

There is no universal cutoff. A dog’s size, age, coat, health, acclimation, wind, moisture and the walking surface all affect comfort. Watch for paw lifting, shivering, reluctance to walk or other changes, and shorten or end the outing when they appear.
