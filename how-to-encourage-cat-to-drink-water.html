How to Encourage Your Cat to Drink More Water

Some cats happily sip from a bowl; others seem far more interested in a dripping tap. The goal is not to force a cat to drink, but to make clean water easy to find and appealing while noticing changes that may signal a health problem.

Quick answer: Offer fresh water in several quiet, accessible places; wash bowls daily; try a wide, shallow bowl made from a different material; and consider wet food or a fountain if your cat likes them. If your cat suddenly drinks much less or much more, urinates differently, vomits, becomes lethargic, or stops eating, contact a veterinarian.

Why water intake matters

Water supports digestion, circulation, temperature regulation and many other normal body functions. Cats get moisture both by drinking and from their food. A cat eating wet food may drink less from a bowl than a cat eating mostly dry food, so bowl visits alone do not tell the whole story.

There is no single daily amount that suits every cat. Body size, diet, activity, environment and health all affect water needs. Avoid trying to diagnose dehydration or calculate a treatment amount at home. If you are worried about your cat's intake, ask your veterinarian what is appropriate for that individual animal.

Set up better water stations

1. Put water in more than one place

Place bowls in quiet, easy-to-reach areas where your cat likes to spend time. In a multi-level home, make water available on each level. In a multi-cat household, provide enough separate stations so one cat cannot easily block another from drinking. Keep bowls away from litter trays and busy, noisy walkways.

2. Experiment with bowl shape and material

Some cats prefer a wide, shallow dish that lets them drink without their whiskers brushing the sides. Try a stable ceramic, stainless-steel or glass bowl and observe which one your cat chooses. Individual preferences vary, so there is no need to replace every bowl at once.

3. Keep water fresh and the bowl clean

Refresh the water regularly and wash bowls every day with dish soap, then rinse them thoroughly. Remove slime or residue rather than simply topping up old water. Keep bowls somewhere they will not collect food crumbs, litter dust or cleaning products.

4. Notice where your cat prefers to drink

If your cat repeatedly chooses one safe location, consider placing a proper water bowl there. Do not leave access to sinks, toilets, buckets containing cleaning products, aquariums treated with chemicals, or other unsafe liquids as a substitute for a clean drinking station.

Use food to add moisture

Wet cat food contains substantially more moisture than dry kibble and can contribute to total water intake. If wet food is suitable for your cat, you can discuss a gradual change or mixed-feeding plan with your veterinarian. You may also try mixing a small amount of plain water into food your cat already accepts, increasing it slowly only if your cat continues to eat normally.

Do not make a sudden diet change or allow a cat to go without food because it refuses a newly moistened meal. If your cat has a prescribed diet or a medical condition, ask the veterinary team before changing food or adding anything to it. Avoid salty stock, seasoned broth, onion or garlic flavourings, and milk.

Should you try a cat water fountain?

Some cats enjoy moving water; others prefer a still bowl or are wary of the sound and movement. A fountain is an optional experiment, not a requirement. Introduce it gradually, keep a familiar bowl available, and let your cat choose. Follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions, take it apart for regular washing, and replace filters as directed. A dirty fountain is not an improvement over a clean bowl.

Notice changes and seek veterinary help

Pay attention to your cat's normal pattern: how often you refresh the water, whether the bowl level changes, appetite, energy, and litter-box habits. A sudden increase in thirst or urination can be just as important as drinking less. Vomiting, diarrhoea, weakness, unusual tiredness, poor appetite, or signs of discomfort also deserve attention.

When to call a veterinarian: Contact your veterinary clinic promptly if your cat is drinking noticeably less or more than usual, is not eating, seems unwell, or has a change in urination. Repeated straining to urinate, producing little or no urine, or crying in the litter box can be an emergency—seek urgent veterinary care rather than waiting.

Never force water into your cat's mouth with a syringe unless a veterinarian has specifically instructed you and shown you how. An animal that is weak, distressed or unable to swallow normally can inhale liquid. A veterinarian can assess hydration, look for an underlying cause and advise on safe treatment.

A simple daily water checklist

  • Refresh each water station and check that it is easy to reach.
  • Wash bowls and rinse away all soap residue.
  • Make sure another pet is not guarding or blocking access.
  • Notice changes in drinking, appetite, energy and litter-box habits.
  • Keep your cat's usual food routine unless a veterinarian advises a change.
