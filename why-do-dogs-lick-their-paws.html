Why Does My Dog Keep Licking Their Paws?

A little paw licking can be normal grooming. Repeated licking, chewing, or focusing on one foot can be a sign of itchiness, irritation, injury, or pain. The pattern—and what the paw looks like—can help you decide what to do next.

Quick answer: Dogs may lick their paws to clean them, but frequent or intense licking can be linked to allergies, irritation, a small injury, parasites, infection, pain, or stress. Check the paw gently. If licking continues, causes skin damage, or comes with limping, swelling, discharge, or a strong smell, contact your veterinarian.

When paw licking is normal

Dogs use their mouths to explore and groom. A brief lick after a walk, after eating, or when cleaning away a little dirt may be ordinary. It is more concerning when your dog returns to the same paw again and again, chews intensely, cannot be distracted, wakes up to lick, or creates red, damp, sore, or hairless skin.

Common reasons dogs lick their paws

1. Allergies or skin irritation

Environmental allergens, contact irritants, and some food-related sensitivities can make paws itchy. You may also notice licking on several feet, scratching elsewhere, or recurring ear and skin problems. A veterinarian can help work out the cause; do not start an elimination diet or medication without professional guidance.

2. A small injury or something stuck

A thorn, grass seed, splinter, tiny cut, insect sting, cracked pad, or damaged nail can make one paw suddenly uncomfortable. Licking that starts abruptly and focuses on one foot deserves a careful look.

3. Infection or inflammation

Repeated moisture and irritation can damage the skin. Redness, swelling, discharge, a strong or unusual smell, sores, or brown saliva staining may indicate a problem that needs veterinary assessment. Do not assume you can identify a yeast or bacterial infection by appearance alone.

4. Pain in the paw or leg

Dogs sometimes lick an area that hurts. Watch for limping, stiffness, reluctance to jump or use stairs, holding a paw up, or reacting when the leg or foot is touched. Pain may not be obvious from the skin surface.

5. Stress, boredom, or a repetitive habit

Some dogs lick to self-soothe, particularly during stressful periods or when they are under-stimulated. However, medical causes such as itchiness and pain should be considered first. Behavioural and physical factors can also occur together.

How to check your dog's paw safely

  1. Choose a calm moment and let your dog settle. Stop if handling causes pain or distress.
  2. Look at the top and underside of the paw, between the toes, around the pads, and around each nail.
  3. Check for redness, swelling, cuts, broken nails, damp skin, discharge, odour, or visible debris.
  4. If the paw is simply dirty and there is no open wound, gently rinse with clean lukewarm water and dry carefully, including between the toes.
  5. Note when the licking began, whether it affects one paw or several, and any changes in walking, appetite, or energy.
Do not dig into the skin. If something is embedded, difficult to remove, or painful, leave it in place and contact a veterinary clinic.

When to contact a veterinarian

Arrange a veterinary appointment if licking is persistent or repeatedly returns, or if you notice redness, swelling, hair loss, sores, a bad smell, discharge, a damaged nail, or limping. Sudden severe pain, inability to put weight on the leg, heavy bleeding, a deep wound, rapidly increasing swelling, or suspected chemical exposure needs urgent veterinary advice.

If your dog seems otherwise well but keeps licking, do not wait until the skin is raw. Early assessment can help identify the underlying cause and prevent further irritation.

What not to do

  • Do not apply human creams, essential oils, disinfectants, or pain-relief products unless your veterinarian specifically recommends them.
  • Do not give human painkillers or leftover pet medication.
  • Do not tightly wrap the paw or force your dog to wear a boot if it causes distress.
  • Do not punish or scold licking. It is a sign to investigate, not misbehaviour.

Helpful everyday habits

After walks, check paws for mud, burrs, grit, and small cuts. During seasons when grass or pollen bothers your dog, ask your veterinarian whether a gentle paw rinse after outdoor time is suitable. Keep nails at a comfortable length and provide regular exercise, sniffing, play, and rest. These habits support comfort but do not replace diagnosis when licking persists.
