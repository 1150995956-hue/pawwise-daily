DOG ENRICHMENT · 8 MIN READ

10 easy indoor enrichment games to keep your dog busy

Rainy day, small apartment, or a quiet afternoon at home? A few minutes of sniffing, searching and learning can make indoor time more interesting for many dogs. You do not need expensive equipment: start with safe household items and a little of your dog’s usual food.

Start with your dog, not the game. Choose activities that match your dog’s age, confidence, mobility and chewing habits. Keep sessions short and enjoyable. Enrichment should offer choice and curiosity—not frustration or pressure.

Before you begin: four simple safety rules

  • Supervise closely. Watch your dog with boxes, towels, toys and food puzzles. Remove anything that is being shredded or swallowed.
  • Use dog-safe food. The easiest reward is part of your dog’s normal daily food allowance. Introduce unfamiliar foods cautiously and check with your veterinarian if your dog has allergies, a medical condition or a prescribed diet.
  • Keep it easy at first. Let your dog succeed quickly. If they become frustrated, make the task simpler or take a break.
  • Make space safe. Use a non-slip surface, keep small objects and electrical cords away, and avoid games that encourage jumping or hard twisting—especially for puppies, seniors or dogs with mobility problems.

10 low-cost games to try at home

GAME 01 · SNIFFING2–5 minutes

1. The treat treasure hunt

Ask your dog to wait in another room or have someone gently hold their harness. Place a few pieces of their usual kibble in easy-to-find spots around one safe room. Release your dog with a cheerful cue such as “Find it.” Start with visible pieces, then make the hiding places slightly less obvious as your dog understands the game.

Make it easier: Put the food on the floor in plain sight. Make it harder: Hide pieces beside furniture legs or behind a safe, stable object—not inside places your dog could get stuck.

GAME 02 · FORAGING3–5 minutes

2. The towel roll

Lay a clean towel flat, scatter a small handful of kibble across it, then loosely fold or roll the towel so food is easy to uncover. Let your dog sniff and nudge it open while you supervise. Use a towel only if your dog does not chew or eat fabric.

Safety note: Skip this game for dogs that swallow cloth, pull threads or become possessive around food. Never leave a fabric puzzle unattended.

GAME 03 · PROBLEM-SOLVING2–4 minutes

3. The open-box search

Put a few kibble pieces in a clean, empty cardboard box with all staples, tape and labels removed. Leave the top fully open and let your dog investigate. For a beginner, use a large box with no flaps to push through.

Safety note: Supervise every moment and remove the box if your dog eats cardboard. Do not use boxes with plastic film, sharp edges, staples or packing materials.

GAME 04 · CHOICE2–3 minutes

4. Which hand?

Place one piece of kibble in one closed hand. Offer both closed hands at your dog’s nose level and wait for a gentle sniff or touch. Open the correct hand and reward. Switch hands between turns. Keep your hands relaxed and never tease or make your dog paw forcefully.

GAME 05 · TRAINING1–3 minutes

5. Teach a simple new cue

Choose one easy behaviour, such as touching your open palm with their nose. Present your hand a few inches away; when your dog investigates or touches it, say “Yes” and reward. Repeat a few times, then stop while your dog is still interested. A new cue is a tiny learning project, not an obedience test.

GAME 06 · SCENT WORK2–5 minutes

6. The cup find

Place one piece of kibble under one of two sturdy, lightweight, non-breakable cups while your dog watches. Invite them to find it. Keep the cups upright and allow your dog to nudge rather than paw hard. If your dog mouths or breaks the cups, stop and choose another game.

GAME 07 · CALM PLAY2–5 minutes

7. Find your toy

Show your dog a familiar toy, place it a short distance away in plain sight, and say “Find your toy.” Praise them when they reach it. Gradually place it partly behind a safe object. Do not hide toys where your dog must climb, jump or squeeze into tight spaces.

GAME 08 · BODY AWARENESS1–2 minutes

8. Follow the slow hand

Hold a treat near your dog’s nose and slowly move your hand a short distance left or right, inviting them to follow with their nose. Reward a gentle turn. Keep movements small and comfortable; do not spin your dog in circles or ask for movements that cause discomfort.

GAME 09 · SETTLING1–3 minutes

9. Relax on a mat

Put a comfortable mat on a quiet, non-slip floor. When your dog steps onto it or chooses to lie down, calmly place a piece of kibble between their paws. Reward relaxed moments without repeatedly asking for a position. Let your dog leave whenever they want.

GAME 10 · SOCIAL CONNECTION2–4 minutes

10. The name-and-reward game

Say your dog’s name once in a friendly voice when they are not distracted. When they look toward you, say “Yes” and reward. Try a few repetitions in different quiet rooms. If they do not respond, move closer or reduce distractions instead of repeating the name louder.

How to build a simple indoor routine

You do not need to do all ten games in one day. Try one sniffing game and one short training or settling activity, then give your dog time to rest. Rotate activities across the week so they stay fresh. On days when your dog is unwell, recovering from surgery or showing pain, ask your veterinarian what activity is appropriate.

Example 10-minute session:
2 minutes of treat treasure hunt → 3 minutes of towel or box search (only if safe for your dog) → 2 minutes practicing a familiar cue → 3 minutes of calm rest. Stop sooner if your dog loses interest or seems tired.

How do you know if a game is a good fit?

Look for relaxed body language, voluntary participation and an easy return to rest. If your dog freezes, repeatedly walks away, pants heavily when not hot, shows escalating frustration, guards the food, or tries to swallow game materials, calmly end the activity and choose something simpler. Sudden behaviour changes or signs of pain deserve veterinary advice.

What indoor games should you avoid?

  • Games with small objects that could be swallowed or become choking hazards.
  • Unsupervised fabric, cardboard or plastic puzzles for dogs that chew and ingest materials.
  • Repeated high jumps, slippery-floor chasing or rough wrestling in a cramped room.
  • Food challenges that make your dog panic, guard resources or become frustrated.
  • Using human foods, supplements or treats that are unsafe for dogs.

Frequently asked questions

Can indoor games replace walks?

Usually not. Enrichment can complement appropriate exercise, toilet breaks and outdoor experiences, but it does not automatically replace them. Follow your veterinarian’s advice about activity for your dog’s age and health.

How often should I play enrichment games?

There is no single schedule for every dog. Several short, positive opportunities can work better than one long session. Watch your dog’s interest and energy, and make sure games do not replace sleep and quiet time.

What if my dog does not understand a puzzle?

Make it easier. Show the food, remove extra steps and reward any safe investigation. If your dog is not interested, try a different activity. The goal is enjoyable engagement, not solving a difficult puzzle.
