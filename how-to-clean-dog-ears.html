How to clean a dog’s ears safely: a gentle, practical guide

A little ear care can help you notice changes early, but more cleaning is not always better. Many dogs with healthy ears need very little routine cleaning. The safest approach is to check first, use a dog-specific cleaner only when appropriate, and leave sore or inflamed ears to your veterinarian.

The short answer: Check your dog’s ears regularly, but clean them only when there is visible dirt or wax buildup or your veterinarian recommends it. Use a veterinary-formulated dog ear cleaner and cotton balls or gauze. Never push cotton swabs into the ear canal. If an ear is red, painful, swollen, or has unusual discharge or a strong odor, contact your veterinarian before cleaning.

1. Do dogs need regular ear cleaning?

Not all dogs do. Some dogs keep their ears clean naturally and may rarely need an ear-cleaning solution. Others may need a tailored routine because of their ear shape, allergies, frequent swimming, or a history of recurring ear problems. Your veterinarian can help decide what is appropriate for your dog.

A healthy ear is generally pale pink, comfortable when gently handled, and free of strong odor or significant discharge. A small amount of wax can be normal. Do not assume that every bit of wax means the ear needs a deep clean.

Look at both ears during ordinary grooming. Notice whether your dog suddenly pulls away when you touch an ear, scratches more than usual, or starts shaking their head repeatedly. These changes can have several causes, including irritation, allergies, parasites, a foreign object, or infection; cleaning alone may not solve the problem.

2. What you need to clean dog ears

  • A dog-specific ear-cleaning solution recommended by your veterinarian. Choose a veterinary-formulated product rather than a homemade mixture.
  • Cotton balls or soft gauze for wiping the visible ear flap and the part of the canal your fingertip can comfortably reach.
  • A clean towel to catch drips and help tidy up after your dog shakes their head.
  • Small treats to make handling calmer and more rewarding.

Have everything ready before you begin. Choose a quiet place where your dog can stand or sit comfortably. If your dog is frightened, painful, or struggling, do not restrain them and force the procedure; pause and ask your veterinary team for help.

3. How to clean your dog’s ears, step by step

Use these steps only if your dog’s ears look comfortable and your veterinarian has not told you to avoid cleaning. Follow the cleaner’s label and your veterinarian’s specific directions if they differ.

  1. Help your dog relax. Let them sniff the bottle, offer a treat, and gently touch the outside of the ear. Keep the first session short if ear handling is new.
  2. Look before you clean. Check for redness, swelling, wounds, discharge, a strong smell, or signs of pain. If you see any of these, stop and call your veterinarian instead of trying to wash the problem away.
  3. Lift the ear flap gently. If your dog is comfortable, hold the ear flap up to expose the opening. Do not pull hard or insert the bottle tip into the canal.
  4. Apply the cleaner as directed. Place the nozzle near the opening without touching the ear. Apply the amount recommended by the product instructions or your veterinarian. Do not forcefully squeeze liquid into the canal.
  5. Massage the base of the ear. Gently massage below the opening for about 20–30 seconds, if your dog tolerates it. You may hear a soft squishing sound as the solution loosens debris.
  6. Let your dog shake. Step back and allow them to shake their head. This helps move loosened material toward the opening.
  7. Wipe only what you can safely reach. Use cotton or gauze to wipe the inner ear flap and visible debris at the entrance. Do not push your finger deep into the canal; stay within the reach of your fingertip or about one knuckle.
  8. Reward and reassess. Praise your dog, offer a treat, and stop if they show discomfort. Repeat on the other ear only if it also needs cleaning and remains comfortable.

For dogs with a history of ear disease, your veterinarian may prescribe a different technique or schedule. Some infected ears are too painful to clean at home until treatment has reduced the inflammation.

4. What not to put in a dog’s ears

  • No cotton swabs or Q-tips inside the canal. They can push debris deeper and injure delicate ear structures or the eardrum.
  • No hydrogen peroxide or rubbing alcohol. These can irritate sensitive or inflamed ear tissue.
  • No vinegar, essential oils, or improvised home remedies. A mixture that seems gentle may sting or worsen an already irritated ear.
  • No forceful flushing. Do not insert the bottle tip or squeeze liquid under pressure into the canal.
  • No aggressive hair plucking or scraping. Ask your veterinarian or a qualified groomer whether any hair around the ear opening needs attention.

Over-cleaning can irritate the ear canal too. The goal is not to make the inside of the ear spotless; it is to remove appropriate buildup without damaging the skin or disrupting the ear’s normal environment.

Important: Do not clean an ear that is red, swollen, very itchy, painful, bleeding, or producing unusual discharge or a strong odor unless your veterinarian has specifically instructed you to do so. A damaged eardrum or infection can change which cleaners are safe.

5. When should you call a veterinarian?

Arrange a veterinary examination if you notice one or more of these signs:

  • Repeated head shaking or persistent ear scratching
  • Redness, swelling, heat, or tenderness
  • Dark, yellow, bloody, or pus-like discharge
  • A strong, musty, or unpleasant odor
  • Scabs, sores, or hair loss around the ear
  • Your dog cries, flinches, or will not let you touch the ear

Head tilt, loss of balance, unusual eye movements, marked lethargy, or severe pain deserve prompt veterinary attention. Do not wait to see whether an ear-cleaning session helps.

Ear infections can involve yeast, bacteria, mites, allergies, or other underlying problems. A veterinarian may examine the canal with an otoscope and test a sample of the discharge to identify the cause. That matters because different causes need different treatments; an ear cleaner alone is not a reliable treatment for an infection.

6. How often should you clean your dog’s ears?

There is no single schedule that suits every dog. A dog with healthy, clean ears may need only occasional checks and no routine solution. A dog that swims often or has recurring ear problems may need a veterinarian-directed plan. Some dogs need cleaning more often during treatment, but that should be based on their diagnosis and instructions.

After swimming or bathing, gently dry the outside of the ears with a towel and keep water from entering the canal. Ask your veterinarian whether a drying or maintenance product is suitable if your dog is prone to ear trouble. Avoid adding a product after every swim unless it is recommended for your dog.

7. Common questions about dog ear cleaning

Can I clean my dog’s ears with water?

Do not pour plain water into the ear canal. Use a veterinary-formulated dog ear cleaner when cleaning is appropriate. A damp cloth can be used on the outer ear flap, but keep moisture out of the canal.

Is brown ear wax always an infection?

No. Wax color alone cannot confirm an infection. A change in the amount or smell of wax, redness, itching, pain, or discharge should be assessed in context by a veterinarian.

My dog shakes their head after cleaning. Is that normal?

A brief shake after cleaner is applied can help move loosened fluid and debris outward. Repeated or persistent head shaking, pain, or distress is not something to ignore; stop and contact your veterinarian.

Can I use human ear drops on my dog?

No. Do not use human ear drops or leftover medication unless a veterinarian has prescribed that exact product for your dog. The right treatment depends on the condition and whether the eardrum is intact.
