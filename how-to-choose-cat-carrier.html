How to Choose a Cat Carrier: Size, Safety and Training

A good carrier should be secure enough to prevent escapes, comfortable enough for your cat to settle, and practical for you to carry and clean. Choosing it before an appointment—and making it familiar at home—can make necessary trips less stressful.

Quick answer: Choose a sturdy carrier with a stable base, secure fastenings, good ventilation and enough room for your cat to stand, turn around and lie comfortably. A front opening plus a top opening or removable top can make everyday use easier. Leave it open at home and build positive experiences gradually rather than bringing it out only on veterinary-visit days.

1. Choose the right size—not just the label

Carrier labels such as small, medium and large are not consistent between manufacturers. Compare the usable interior dimensions with your own cat instead of relying on the label or the carrier’s outside measurements.

Your cat should be able to stand in a natural position without being pressed against the roof, turn around, and lie down comfortably. The carrier should also be stable enough that your cat is not sliding around every time it moves. Remember that a thick cushion or blanket takes up space, so check the fit with the bedding you intend to use.

Check the manufacturer's maximum weight rating, but do not treat weight as a substitute for fit. Two cats with the same weight can have different body lengths and builds. If you are buying for a growing kitten, choose a size that will remain suitable as the kitten grows, while still providing a secure, stable space now.

2. Compare carrier styles and access

Rigid-sided carriers

A sturdy plastic carrier is often a practical choice for routine veterinary trips. It can provide a firm base, is usually straightforward to wipe clean, and some models separate into a top and bottom half. A removable top can be especially useful: ask your veterinary team whether your cat may be examined while remaining in the familiar lower half.

Soft-sided carriers

A soft carrier may suit a calm cat and a particular journey, but check that its floor stays level, the sides do not collapse onto your cat, the mesh is secure, and every zip closes completely. It should not sag so much that your cat loses usable space. Follow the transport provider's rules if you are travelling by air or another service.

Openings matter

A front door lets a carrier-trained cat walk in voluntarily. A top opening can make gentle placement easier when needed. A design with both, or with a fully removable top, gives you more options than a narrow front-only opening. Look closely at the product photographs: a small top hatch is not the same as a top half that comes away.

Do not choose a carrier only because it looks fashionable or is easy to store. The shape, access, stability and security matter more than appearance. Some backpack-style carriers may not give a cat enough room to change position or may move noticeably while you walk; assess the actual interior and stability rather than assuming every backpack is suitable.

3. Check every closure, the ventilation and the base

Before buying, open and close the door several times. Check the latch, hinges, clips, seams, zips, handle and any connection between the top and bottom. Nothing should be cracked, loose, bent or easy to push open. With a soft carrier, inspect the mesh and stitching for gaps or damage. With a rigid carrier, make sure the two halves fit together securely and that the door cannot pop out of its frame.

Look for ventilation openings on more than one side and make sure they will remain unobstructed when the carrier is placed in its usual travel position. Openings should allow airflow without leaving sharp edges or gaps where a paw or claw could become trapped. A carrier that feels enclosed but has poor airflow is not a safe choice.

The base should stay flat and support your cat's weight without bending or tipping. Lift the empty carrier by its normal handle and check that the handle and attachments feel secure. If you can do so safely, test the carrier with an appropriate load before relying on it; never put your cat inside while testing an unfamiliar or questionable handle.

4. Plan for cleaning and comfort

Travel can involve drool, shed fur, urine, stool or vomit, even when a cat is normally tidy. Choose surfaces you can reach and clean, and check whether removable bedding can be washed and dried fully. A leak-resistant floor with a washable absorbent towel or pad can make a trip easier to manage.

Place familiar, non-slip bedding inside so your cat has a comfortable surface. Bring a spare towel or absorbent pad for longer journeys. If you cover part of the carrier to reduce visual stimulation, keep every ventilation opening clear and watch that the carrier does not become warm. Never leave a cat in a parked vehicle.

After use, follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions. Cats Protection recommends mild, non-toxic soap and water, followed by thorough rinsing and drying. Avoid leaving strong cleaning smells behind; do not mix cleaning chemicals. Replace a carrier if its shell is cracked, its door or fasteners no longer work reliably, or its fabric and seams cannot be cleaned safely.

5. Help your cat get used to the carrier before you need it

If the carrier appears only when it is time for a stressful trip, your cat may learn to avoid it. Instead, make it an ordinary part of the home. The feline-care guidance from Cat Friendly Homes and the AAFP/ISFM recommends building positive associations and allowing the cat to approach at its own pace.

  1. Leave it open. Put the carrier in a quiet room your cat already likes. Secure the door open or remove it if the design allows, so it cannot swing shut and frighten your cat.
  2. Add familiar bedding. Use a blanket or towel your cat already rests on. Keep the carrier steady and place it where your cat can approach and leave freely.
  3. Reward curiosity. Put a few treats or a favourite toy near the entrance, then just inside. Let your cat decide whether to go in. Do not push or drag the cat into the carrier.
  4. Build up slowly. When your cat is comfortable entering, briefly move the door while offering a reward, then open it again. Gradually practise closing it for a few seconds, lifting the carrier a little, and carrying it a short distance—only while your cat remains relaxed.
  5. Practise ordinary trips. If your cat is comfortable, occasionally take a short, calm practice trip that does not always end at the veterinary clinic. Return home and reward calm behaviour. Stop and make the next session easier if your cat becomes distressed.

There is no fixed timetable. Some cats progress quickly; others need many short sessions over days or weeks. Hissing, frantic struggling, panting or repeated attempts to escape are signs to pause and reassess rather than force the next step. For a cat with severe travel fear, ask your veterinarian for an individual plan well before the next non-urgent appointment.

6. Use the carrier safely in a vehicle

For car travel, keep your cat inside a closed carrier for the entire journey, including while doors are being opened at stops. A loose cat can distract the driver, move into the footwell or escape when a door opens. Keep the carrier upright and stable, away from direct sun and strong heater or air-conditioning airflow.

Follow the carrier and vehicle manufacturers' restraint instructions. Cat Friendly Homes notes that a carrier should be seat-belted in the back seat only if that specific carrier has been crash-tested for that use; otherwise, its guidance is to place it on the floor of the back seat. Do not assume that a handle, strap or ordinary seat belt makes every carrier crash-safe.

For flights, trains, buses and cross-border journeys, check the operator's current pet and carrier rules before booking. Requirements can differ by route, carrier size, materials and whether the animal travels in the cabin or elsewhere. Ask your veterinarian whether your cat is fit to travel, especially if your cat is very young, elderly, unwell or has a short-nosed breed's breathing concerns. Do not give sedatives or travel medication unless your veterinarian has advised you for that cat.

Never leave a cat unattended in a parked car. If your cat becomes unwell, overheated, has trouble breathing or is injured during travel, seek veterinary help promptly.

7. A before-you-buy checklist

  • My cat can stand, turn around and lie down comfortably inside.
  • The internal dimensions and weight limit suit my individual cat.
  • The base stays level and does not collapse or bend under normal use.
  • The door, latch, hinges, clips, zips and handle are secure and undamaged.
  • Ventilation is available on more than one side and will not be blocked.
  • The carrier has practical front and/or top access; a removable top is a useful bonus.
  • The interior and bedding can be cleaned and dried thoroughly.
  • The carrier suits the actual journey and the transport provider's rules.
  • I can leave it open at home and practise with it before a trip.

Frequently asked questions

Is a hard or soft cat carrier better?

There is no single answer for every cat and journey. A sturdy rigid carrier with a removable top is often practical for veterinary visits because it has a firm base and can be easier to clean and access. A well-constructed soft carrier may work for some cats and routes if it remains stable, ventilated and securely closed. Choose by fit, construction and the journey—not by style alone.

Can two cats share one carrier?

For routine transport, use a separate, correctly sized carrier for each cat. Even cats that live together may become frightened or uncomfortable during travel, and separate carriers make it easier to keep each animal secure and give the veterinary team access. Ask your veterinarian for advice if there is a special medical or mobility reason you are considering another arrangement.

Should I cover the carrier with a towel?

A light cover over part of the carrier may help some cats feel less exposed. Keep ventilation openings completely clear, avoid direct sun and heat, and check that the carrier remains cool and well ventilated.

What if my cat refuses to go inside?

For future non-urgent trips, start carrier training well in advance with the door open, familiar bedding and small rewards. Do not chase or force a frightened cat as a routine strategy. If a trip is urgent or your cat cannot be handled safely, call your veterinary clinic for advice on the safest way to transport them.

Remember: A carrier is safety equipment, not just a travel accessory. Inspect it before every trip, keep your cat secured inside, and ask a veterinarian for help with severe fear, motion sickness or health concerns.
