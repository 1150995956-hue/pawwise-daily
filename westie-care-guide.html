West Highland White Terrier care: a practical guide to grooming, skin and daily life

Westies are small, sturdy terriers with big personalities. Their bright white double coat, curious nature and independent streak are part of their charm—but they also mean that thoughtful grooming, consistent training and regular health checks matter. This guide explains the everyday basics without treating every dog as if it has the same needs.

1. Understand the Westie coat

The West Highland White Terrier has a distinctive double coat: a relatively harsh outer coat over a softer undercoat. That coat is not simply decorative; it has specific maintenance needs. A pet trim and a traditional hand-stripped coat are different grooming approaches. If you prefer the traditional texture or show presentation, ask an experienced Westie groomer about hand-stripping. Many family pets are clipped for easier maintenance.

2. Brush regularly and plan professional grooming

Brush and comb your Westie several times a week, paying attention to the legs, belly, beard, behind the ears and other places where tangles can form. Work gently from the ends of a tangle rather than pulling hard against the skin. Check the coat for burrs, dirt, mats and small skin changes after walks.

Many pet Westies are professionally groomed roughly every four to six weeks, although the right interval depends on coat length, style and how quickly the coat grows. Ask the groomer to show you a simple between-visit routine. Keep nails at a comfortable length and check the ears, paws and teeth as part of normal handling.

Bathing tip: Avoid bathing on an arbitrary daily or weekly schedule. Bathe when needed with a dog-appropriate product, rinse thoroughly and dry the coat well. If your dog has itchy, inflamed or recurring skin problems, ask your veterinarian which shampoo is suitable before experimenting with products.

3. Take skin irritation seriously

Westies can be prone to allergic skin disease. Repeated scratching, paw licking, face rubbing, redness, recurring ear trouble, scabs or hair loss deserve attention. These signs do not prove that food is the cause: environmental allergens, parasites, infections and other conditions can look similar.

Keep a simple note of when symptoms happen, which areas are affected and any recent changes in food, treats, grooming products or environment. Share that history with your veterinarian. Do not start a restrictive diet, use human medication or repeatedly switch shampoos without professional guidance. A veterinarian can work out whether testing, parasite control, treatment or a properly planned diet trial is appropriate.

4. Give your Westie daily movement and sniffing time

Westies were developed as working terriers and often enjoy exploring, chasing toys and using their noses. Offer regular walks, sniffing opportunities, short play sessions and age-appropriate games. The right amount depends on your individual dog's age, fitness, health and weather conditions; build activity gradually and allow rest.

Because terriers may chase small animals or moving objects, use a leash outside unfenced areas and choose a secure enclosed space for off-leash play. In warm weather, avoid the hottest part of the day, provide water and stop if your dog seems overheated or unusually tired.

5. Make training short, upbeat and consistent

A Westie's intelligence can come with a strong independent streak. Use reward-based training, small treats from the daily food allowance, and sessions that finish before your dog loses interest. Useful skills include coming when called, walking comfortably on a leash, settling on a mat, waiting at doors and leaving unsafe objects alone.

Do not rely on punishment or repeated shouting. If barking, digging or chasing becomes difficult to manage, provide appropriate outlets and seek help from a qualified reward-based trainer. Make sure everyone in the household uses the same cues and rules.

6. Keep up with veterinary and dental care

Arrange routine veterinary examinations and ask about vaccinations, parasite prevention, dental care, weight and any breed-relevant screening. Breed organizations and veterinary references note that Westies may be affected by several conditions, including skin disease and some inherited eye, joint or other disorders. This does not mean your dog will develop them; it means regular preventive care and early discussion of changes are worthwhile.

Brush your dog's teeth with toothpaste made for dogs, ideally as part of a regular routine. Never use human toothpaste. Contact your veterinarian about persistent bad breath, gum redness, broken teeth, chewing changes, eye redness or squinting, recurring ear odor, limping, persistent coughing or changes in breathing.

Seek urgent veterinary help for difficulty breathing, collapse, seizures, severe bleeding, repeated unproductive retching or suspected poisoning. If your Westie suddenly cannot walk normally or appears to be in severe pain, contact a veterinary clinic promptly.

7. A realistic daily Westie routine

  • Morning: Fresh water, measured meal if scheduled, toilet break and a walk with time to sniff.
  • During the day: Rest, a short reward-based training session or a safe food puzzle, plus a toilet break appropriate to age and needs.
  • Evening: Another walk or play session, a quick coat and paw check, and calm time with the family.
  • Several times a week: Thorough brushing and combing; check ears, nails, skin and teeth as tolerated.
  • Regularly: Groomer appointments and preventive veterinary care tailored to your dog.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I brush a Westie?

Several times a week is a useful starting point. Dogs with longer coats or tangling may need more frequent brushing. Keep sessions gentle and positive.

How often does a Westie need professional grooming?

Many pet Westies visit a groomer about every four to six weeks. The best schedule depends on the coat style, growth and your ability to maintain the coat between visits.

Are Westies prone to skin allergies?

They can be, but itching has many possible causes. Persistent or recurring symptoms should be assessed by a veterinarian rather than treated through repeated product or diet changes at home.

Are Westies good apartment dogs?

They can adapt to different homes when their needs are met. Daily walks, sniffing, play, training and appropriate outlets for terrier instincts matter more than having a large house.
