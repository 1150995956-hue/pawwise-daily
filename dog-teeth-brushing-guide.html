How to brush your dog’s teeth: a gentle step-by-step guide

Tooth brushing can feel unfamiliar to a dog at first. The goal is not to finish a perfect brushing session on day one. Build comfort in small steps, use supplies made for pets, and ask your veterinarian to check your dog’s mouth regularly.

Updated November 19, 2026 · Pawwise Daily Editorial Team. This educational guide is not individually veterinary reviewed and does not replace advice from your veterinarian.

Why dental care matters

Plaque collects on teeth and can harden into tartar. Dental disease may cause discomfort, gum inflammation, loose or damaged teeth, and difficulty eating. Dogs do not always show obvious signs of mouth pain, so routine veterinary dental checks are important even when your dog seems happy.

The American Veterinary Medical Association recommends that a veterinarian check your pet’s teeth and gums at least once a year. Your dog may need an earlier examination if you notice persistent bad breath, red or bleeding gums, loose or broken teeth, unusual drooling, dropping food, or reluctance to eat.

What you need

  • A soft-bristled pet toothbrush or a small, soft toothbrush that fits your dog comfortably.
  • Toothpaste specifically formulated for dogs.
  • Small rewards and a calm, unhurried place.
Important: Do not use human toothpaste. Dogs swallow what is put in their mouths, and human toothpaste is not designed for them. Never use baking soda or household dental products as a substitute for pet toothpaste.

Introduce brushing in small steps

Step 1: Make mouth handling feel normal

Choose a quiet moment when your dog is relaxed. Briefly touch the side of the muzzle, praise your dog, and offer a reward. Repeat for a few seconds at a time. Stop while your dog is still comfortable. If your dog pulls away, growls, snaps, or seems worried, do not force the interaction.

Step 2: Gently lift the lip

When muzzle touches are easy, briefly lift the lip to look at the teeth. Reward calm behaviour and keep sessions short. You do not need to open your dog’s mouth wide to begin.

Step 3: Introduce the pet toothpaste

Let your dog sniff the toothbrush and taste a tiny amount of dog toothpaste from your finger or the brush, following the product directions. Pair this with praise. The aim is to make the new taste and tool familiar.

Step 4: Brush a few teeth

Start with just a few outer tooth surfaces, especially the teeth along the cheek side. Use gentle, small movements and a soft brush. Gradually add more teeth over several sessions as your dog accepts the routine. Never wrestle the mouth open or continue if your dog appears painful or distressed.

Step 5: Build a repeatable routine

Daily brushing is the ideal goal. If that is not realistic at first, establish a calm routine and ask your veterinarian what level of home care is appropriate for your dog. Consistency matters more than attempting a long session that your dog fears.

What if your dog will not accept a toothbrush?

Go back to an easier step and progress more slowly. Some dogs need more time or a different brush shape. If handling the mouth is unsafe, stop and ask your veterinary team for help. Your veterinarian may discuss other options, such as products carrying the Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC) seal, dental diets, or dental chews suited to your dog. These options should be selected with professional advice and do not replace a veterinary examination.

When to book a dental check

Arrange a veterinary appointment if you notice ongoing bad breath, red or swollen gums, visible tartar, mouth bleeding, pawing at the face, unusual drooling, chewing on one side, dropping food, or a change in appetite. A dog with a painful mouth may resist handling, so do not try to scrape tartar off at home.

Professional care: A proper veterinary dental assessment may require examination and cleaning below the gumline, sometimes with dental X-rays and anesthesia. “Anesthesia-free” scraping cannot provide the same assessment or treatment. Ask your veterinarian to explain the recommended plan and its risks and benefits for your dog.

A simple weekly habit tracker

  • Choose a regular, calm time for dental care.
  • Use only a dog-safe toothbrush and dog toothpaste.
  • Reward cooperation and keep each session positive.
  • Record any new mouth or eating changes.
  • Keep up with veterinary dental checks.
