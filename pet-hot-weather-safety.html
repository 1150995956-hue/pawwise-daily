How to Keep Dogs and Cats Safe in Hot Weather

Hot weather does not arrive at the same time everywhere. Whether you are facing a summer heatwave, a humid tropical afternoon, or an unusually warm day, a few simple habits can help protect the animals who share your home.

Quick answer: Keep fresh water available, provide a cool and shaded place to rest, avoid strenuous activity during the hottest part of the day, check walking surfaces, and never leave an animal in a parked vehicle. If you suspect heatstroke, contact a veterinarian or emergency veterinary clinic immediately.

Why heat and humidity matter

Dogs and cats can struggle when their surroundings become too hot. Dogs mainly cool themselves by panting, while cats may groom and seek cooler places. High humidity can make it harder for an animal to lose heat. Heat illness can develop outdoors or inside a poorly ventilated room, vehicle, shed, or conservatory.

There is no single safe temperature for every pet. Age, body condition, coat, breed, health, activity, shade, airflow, and humidity all matter. Pay attention to the conditions where you live rather than relying on a calendar season or one temperature number.

A simple hot-weather routine

1. Make water easy to reach

Put clean, fresh water in stable bowls in places your pet can access. Check and refresh it regularly, especially outdoors where bowls can heat up, collect debris, or be knocked over. Take water and a portable bowl on outings with your dog.

2. Provide a genuinely cool place to rest

Make sure pets can move to shade and a cooler, well-ventilated indoor area. Follow the shade as it moves through the day: a spot that is shaded in the morning may be in direct sun later. Fans or air conditioning can help make an indoor space more comfortable, but never confine an animal in a hot room without a way to move to a cooler area.

3. Change the timing and intensity of exercise

Choose the coolest part of the day for walks and play, reduce the length and intensity when it is hot or humid, and take breaks. On very hot days, skip strenuous exercise and use calm indoor enrichment instead. Let your pet set the pace; do not encourage them to keep going when they slow down or seek shade.

4. Check the ground before walking

Sun-heated pavement, asphalt, sand, and other surfaces can burn paw pads. Choose shaded routes or grass where practical, and avoid surfaces that feel uncomfortably hot to your hand. Remember that a dog may be closer to the hot ground than you are.

5. Never leave a pet in a parked vehicle

A parked vehicle can become dangerously hot very quickly, even if a window is partly open or the vehicle is parked in shade. Do not leave a pet alone in a parked car while you run an errand. Plan trips so your animal can stay safely at home when they cannot accompany you.

Pets that need extra care

Any dog or cat can develop heat-related illness. Be especially cautious with flat-faced breeds, including pugs, bulldogs, and Persian cats; very young or older animals; overweight pets; animals with heart or breathing problems; and pets with thick coats or other health conditions. Ask your veterinarian for advice tailored to your animal, especially before travel, outdoor activity, or a major change in routine.

Warning signs of heat illness

Watch for unusual or heavy panting, excessive drooling, fast or difficult breathing, weakness, unusual tiredness, vomiting, diarrhoea, stumbling, confusion, collapse, or seizures. Cats may show less obvious changes, such as marked weakness, rapid breathing, or hiding. Do not wait for every sign to appear.

Important: Suspected heatstroke is an emergency. Contact a veterinarian or the nearest emergency veterinary clinic immediately and follow their instructions. Do not delay professional care to see whether your pet improves on their own.

What to do if you are worried

Stop exercise and move your pet away from the heat into a cooler, shaded or air-conditioned place. Call a veterinarian or emergency clinic straight away and explain what happened and what signs you can see. Follow their instructions about safe cooling and transport; recommendations can depend on the animal's condition. If possible, have someone call ahead while you arrange prompt transport.

Do not force water into an animal that is weak, confused, vomiting, or unable to swallow normally. Do not give human medicines. Even if your pet seems to recover, a suspected heat illness still needs veterinary advice because complications may not be obvious at first.

Make the plan fit your local climate

In humid tropical regions, pay close attention to humidity and airflow, not just the thermometer. In dry, sunny regions, provide shade and protect paws from hot ground. During travel, check the forecast at every stop and never assume that a comfortable morning will stay comfortable in a parked vehicle. In cooler countries, an unexpected warm spell can still catch pets and owners off guard.
