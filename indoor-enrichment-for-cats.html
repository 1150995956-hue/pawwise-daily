Indoor cat enrichment: 8 easy ways to make every day more interesting

A happy indoor life is about more than toys. Give your cat safe choices to hunt, climb, scratch, hide, watch the world and spend time with you.

Updated November 19, 2026 · Pawwise Daily Editorial Team

Indoor cats can enjoy safe, comfortable lives, but a predictable room with little to explore may leave some cats under-stimulated. Enrichment means giving a cat appropriate opportunities to use natural behaviours: stalking, chasing, pouncing, searching for food, climbing, scratching, hiding and choosing when to interact.

There is no single perfect setup. A confident young cat may enjoy energetic games, while a shy, older or less mobile cat may prefer quiet hiding places and easy-to-reach resting spots. Start small, watch your cat’s response and keep activities positive.

1. Make time for short interactive play

Use a wand toy or soft toy that can be moved like prey: let it travel along the floor, disappear briefly behind a cushion, then move away. Give your cat chances to stalk, chase and catch it. Several short sessions may suit some cats better than one long session; let your cat set the pace and stop if they lose interest or seem tired.

Keep hands and feet out of the game. Use a toy between your hands and your cat, and finish by allowing a catch. If your cat enjoys it, offer a small part of their normal daily food allowance afterwards.

2. Turn part of mealtime into a search game

Try placing a small portion of your cat’s usual measured food in a few easy-to-find spots or using a suitable puzzle feeder. Begin with a simple puzzle and show your cat how it works. Make it easier if they become frustrated.

Count puzzle food and treats as part of the daily ration. Check that your cat is actually eating enough, especially if they are elderly, unwell, underweight or on a prescribed diet. Do not suddenly make food difficult to reach.

3. Offer boxes, tunnels and quiet hiding places

A clean cardboard box, a paper bag with handles removed, or a cat tunnel can become a place to investigate, hide or rest. Put one in a calm area and let your cat approach it voluntarily. A familiar blanket can make a new resting spot more inviting.

Every cat should have a safe retreat where they will not be disturbed. Never pull a hiding cat out to make them play or meet visitors.

4. Add safe climbing and window-watching options

Many cats enjoy looking down from a stable perch. A secure cat tree, sturdy shelf or window seat can add useful vertical space. Make sure furniture cannot wobble or tip, and keep the route up and down manageable. For older cats or cats with mobility limits, provide lower steps or ramps rather than expecting big jumps.

A window view can provide changing sights and sounds. Secure windows and screens so your cat cannot fall or escape, and make sure the perch does not overheat in direct sun.

5. Give your cat more than one scratching surface

Scratching is normal cat behaviour. Offer a stable vertical post and, if your cat prefers it, a horizontal cardboard or sisal scratcher. Place options near sleeping areas or places your cat already likes to scratch. Reward interest with praise or a treat; do not punish scratching.

6. Rotate toys instead of leaving everything out

Keep a small selection available and put some toys away for a few days before bringing them back. Try different textures and movements, but avoid toys with loose pieces that could be chewed off. A simple paper ball may delight one cat while another prefers a soft kicker toy.

7. Adjust enrichment for your cat and household

For kittens, choose sturdy, age-appropriate toys and supervise active play. Older cats may still enjoy play, but gentler movement, shorter sessions and easy access to resources can help. If your cat has arthritis, heart or breathing disease, or another medical condition, ask your veterinarian what level of activity is appropriate.

In homes with more than one cat, provide multiple separated resting, feeding, water, litter and scratching locations. Give each cat a chance to play individually if they prefer it. One cat should not be able to block another from reaching food, litter or a safe resting place.

8. Keep enrichment safe

  • Put away wand toys, string, ribbon, thread, elastic bands and anything that could be swallowed after supervised play.
  • Avoid small detachable parts, sharp edges, plastic bags and toys that are damaged or shedding pieces.
  • If you use a laser toy, finish by directing your cat to a physical toy or treat they can catch; do not leave the game unresolved.
  • Keep food puzzles clean and use materials that cannot splinter or break into swallowable pieces.
  • Secure balconies, windows, shelves and heavy furniture. Check that houseplants are cat-safe before bringing them indoors.
When to ask your veterinarian: A sudden drop in play, new hiding, irritability, unusual vocalising, changes in appetite or toileting, or difficulty jumping can have a medical cause. Do not assume boredom is the explanation. Arrange a veterinary check, especially if the change is sudden or persistent.

A simple 10-minute enrichment routine

2 minutesOffer a short wand-toy game and let your cat catch the toy.
2 minutesHide a few pieces of their measured food in easy places.
3 minutesInvite exploration of a box, tunnel or scratching post.
3 minutesLet your cat choose a perch, window view or quiet rest.

This is only a starting point, not a required schedule. Some cats want more activity; others prefer a little at a time. Consistency and choice matter more than expensive equipment.

Frequently asked questions

How much play does an indoor cat need?

There is no exact number that suits every cat. Offer regular opportunities for short interactive sessions and adjust length and intensity to your cat’s age, health and interest.

Do I need to buy expensive enrichment toys?

No. A sturdy cardboard box, safe paper ball, simple hiding game or homemade puzzle made from safe materials can be useful. Supervise homemade items and discard anything damaged.

My cat ignores toys. What should I do?

Try a different movement, texture or time of day. Some cats prefer slow ground-level movement, food searches, watching birds from a secure window or simply having a quiet hiding place. Never force interaction.
